Basics

Given a set of transactions, find rules that signal the presence of an item based on the presence of other items in the same transaction.

The Walmart “market basket” use case

TID Items
1 Bread, Milk
2 Bread, Diaper, Beer, Eggs
3 Milk, Diaper, Beer, Coke
4 Bread, Milk, Diaper, Beer
5 Bread, Milk, Diaper, Coke

Example rules:

  • \(\{\text{Diaper}\} \to \{\text{Beer}\}\)
  • \(\{\text{Milk}, \text{Bread}\} \to \{\text{Eggs}, \text{Coke}\}\)
  • \(\{\text{Beer}, \text{Bread}\} \to \{\text{Milk}\}\)

Rules discover co-occurrence, not causality.

Association Rules Applications

Association rules are useful for marketing and sales promotion.

  • If \(\{\text{Bagels}, \ldots\} \to \{\text{Potato Chips}\}\), potato chips as the consequent can suggest actions to increase potato chip sales
  • Bagels as the antecedent can suggest which products could be affected if the store interrupted bagel sales

They are also useful for arranging goods.

  • Goal: identify products bought together by a sufficiently large number of customers
  • Method: use receipt data to identify dependencies between products

Frequent Itemsets

An itemset is a collection of one or more items.

  • Example: \(\{\text{Milk}, \text{Bread}, \text{Diaper}\}\)
  • A k-itemset contains \(k\) elements

The support cardinality \(\sigma(X)\) is the number of transactions containing itemset \(X\).

  • Example: \(\sigma(\{\text{Milk}, \text{Bread}, \text{Diaper}\}) = 2\)

The support \(s(X)\) is the fraction of transactions containing \(X\).

  • Example: \(s(\{\text{Milk}, \text{Bread}, \text{Diaper}\}) = 2/5\)

A frequent itemset has support greater than or equal to a threshold minsup.

Association Rules

An association rule is an implication of the form:

\[X \to Y\]

where \(X\) and \(Y\) are itemsets.

Example:

\[\{\text{Milk}, \text{Diaper}\} \to \{\text{Beer}\}\]

Association rules are evaluated using:

  • Support: fraction of transactions that include \(X \cup Y\)
  • Confidence: fraction of transactions containing \(X\) that also contain \(Y\)

For \(\{\text{Milk}, \text{Diaper}\} \to \{\text{Beer}\}\):

\[s = \frac{\sigma(\text{Milk}, \text{Diaper}, \text{Beer})}{|T|} = \frac{2}{5} = 0.4\]

\[c = \frac{\sigma(\text{Milk}, \text{Diaper}, \text{Beer})}{\sigma(\text{Milk}, \text{Diaper})} = \frac{2}{3} = 0.67\]

Support and Confidence: Statistical Interpretation

Support indicates the probability that \(X\) and \(Y\) appear together in a transaction.

\[s(X \to Y)=P(X \cup Y)\]

Confidence for \(X \to Y\) models the conditional probability of \(Y\) when \(X\) is present.

\[c(X \to Y)=P(Y \mid X)=\frac{P(X \cup Y)}{P(X)}\]

Problem Formulation

Given a set of transactions \(T\), find all association rules such that:

  • Support >= minsup
  • Confidence >= minconf

Naive approach:

  • List all possible association rules
  • For each rule, compute support and confidence
  • Remove rules that do not meet minsup and minconf

This is computationally prohibitive.

Searching Association Rules: Two Steps

Association rule discovery is usually divided into two steps.

  1. Generate frequent itemsets
    • Generate all itemsets with support at least minsup
  2. Generate rules
    • For each frequent itemset, generate rules with high confidence
    • Each rule is a binary partition of the itemset

Generating frequent itemsets is still computationally complex.

Frequent Itemsets Generation

Given \(d\) elements, the number of candidate itemsets grows exponentially.

For items \(\{A,B,C,D,E\}\):

Size Candidate itemsets
1 A, B, C, D, E
2 AB, AC, AD, AE, BC, BD, BE, CD, CE, DE
3 ABC, ABD, ABE, ACD, ACE, ADE, BCD, BCE, BDE, CDE
4 ABCD, ABCE, ABDE, ACDE, BCDE
5 ABCDE

The search space contains:

\[\sum_{k=1}^{d}\binom{d}{k}=2^d-1\]

Candidate Reduction

The Apriori principle:

If an itemset is frequent, then all of its subsets must be frequent too.

This follows from the anti-monotonic property of support:

\[\forall X,Y : (X \subseteq Y) \Rightarrow s(X) \geq s(Y)\]

The support of an itemset cannot exceed the support of its subsets.

Visualizing the Apriori Principle

If an itemset is infrequent, none of its supersets can be frequent.

For example, if \(AB\) is infrequent:

  • Supersets such as \(ABC\), \(ABD\), and \(ABE\) cannot be frequent
  • Larger supersets such as \(ABCD\), \(ABCE\), \(ABDE\), and \(ABCDE\) can also be pruned

Apriori uses this property to avoid exploring branches of the itemset lattice that cannot satisfy minsup.

The Apriori Algorithm

Initialize:

\[F_1 = \{i \mid i \in I \land \sigma(\{i\}) \geq N \times \text{minsup}\}\]

Then repeat for \(k=2,3,\ldots\):

Ck = apriori-gen(Fk-1)

for each transaction t in T:
    Ct = subset(Ck, t)
    for each candidate itemset c in Ct:
        sigma(c) = sigma(c) + 1

Fk = {c | c in Ck and sigma(c) >= N * minsup}

Stop when no new frequent itemsets are generated.

Frequent Maximal Itemset

A frequent itemset is maximal if none of its adjacent supersets is frequent.

Maximal frequent itemsets define the border between:

  • Frequent itemsets
  • Infrequent itemsets

They provide a compact representation because all subsets of a maximal frequent itemset are also frequent, but their exact support values are not retained.

Closed Itemset

An itemset is closed if none of its adjacent supersets has the same support.

Closed vs Maximal Itemsets: Minimal Support 2

With minimal support equal to 2:

  • Number of closed frequent itemsets: 9
  • Number of maximal frequent itemsets: 4

Closed itemsets preserve enough information to recover support for all frequent patterns.

Maximal itemsets preserve a more compact description of the largest frequent patterns, but lose detailed support information for their subsets.

Closed vs Maximal Itemsets: Efficiency

From an efficiency point of view, closed and maximal itemsets provide more compact representations than all frequent itemsets.

  • They are fewer than frequent itemsets
  • This matters when the number of itemsets is very high
  • Only closed itemsets determine a lossless compression of frequent patterns
  • Closed itemsets contain complete support information for the frequent itemsets they represent

Closed vs Maximal Itemsets: Semantics

From the semantic point of view:

  • Maximal itemsets are the most complex events that characterize the domain for a given support threshold
  • Non-maximal itemsets can be considered partial descriptions or construction steps of the maximal event
  • Closed itemsets preserve support information and therefore provide a more faithful representation of the frequent-pattern structure

Rule Generation

Given a frequent itemset \(L\), find all non-empty subsets \(f \subset L\) such that:

\[f \to L - f\]

and the rule satisfies the minimum confidence constraint.

For \(L=\{A,B,C,D\}\), candidate rules include:

  • \(ABC \to D\), \(ABD \to C\), \(ACD \to B\), \(BCD \to A\)
  • \(A \to BCD\), \(B \to ACD\), \(C \to ABD\), \(D \to ABC\)
  • \(AB \to CD\), \(AC \to BD\), \(AD \to BC\), \(BC \to AD\), \(BD \to AC\), \(CD \to AB\)

If \(|L|=k\), there are \(2^k-2\) candidate association rules, excluding \(L \to \emptyset\) and \(\emptyset \to L\).

Rule Generation: Confidence Pruning

Confidence is not anti-monotonic with respect to the whole rule.

  • \(c(ABC \to D)\) can be higher or lower than \(c(AB \to D)\)

However, confidence is anti-monotonic with respect to the left-hand side for rules generated from the same itemset.

Given itemset \(Y\) and two rules:

  • \(r_1: X \to Y-X\)
  • \(r_2: X' \to Y-X'\)

with \(X' \subset X \subset Y\), then:

\[c(r_1) \geq c(r_2)\]

Confidence Pruning: Proof

Confidence is defined as:

\[c(r_1)=\frac{\sigma(Y)}{\sigma(X)}\]

\[c(r_2)=\frac{\sigma(Y)}{\sigma(X')}\]

Since \(X' \subset X\), the anti-monotonic support property gives:

\[\sigma(X') \geq \sigma(X)\]

Therefore:

\[c(r_1) \geq c(r_2)\]

For \(L=\{A,B,C,D\}\):

\[c(ABC \to D) \geq c(AB \to CD) \geq c(A \to BCD)\]

Rule Generation Lattice

For itemset \(ABCD\), rules can be organized by consequent size.

Consequent size Candidate rules
0 \(ABCD \to \{\}\)
1 \(BCD \to A\), \(ACD \to B\), \(ABD \to C\), \(ABC \to D\)
2 \(BC \to AD\), \(BD \to AC\), \(CD \to AB\), \(AD \to BC\), \(AC \to BD\), \(AB \to CD\)
3 \(D \to ABC\), \(C \to ABD\), \(B \to ACD\), \(A \to BCD\)

Low-confidence rules prune more general rules below them.

Rule Generation: Tiered Approach

To avoid generating rules with limited confidence, use a tiered approach.

  • Each tier is characterized by an increasing cardinality of the right-hand side
  • Candidate rules are generated by merging two rules that share the same prefix in the right-hand side
  • This avoids generating the same rule multiple times

Example:

  • Rule \(D \to ABC\) can be pruned if \(AD \to BC\) has confidence below minconf

Interestingness

Objective measures prioritize rules based on statistical criteria calculated from data.

  • Many formulas exist
  • Each formula has its own advantages and disadvantages

Subjective measures prioritize rules based on user-defined criteria.

A pattern is interesting if:

  • It contradicts the user’s expectations
  • The user is interested in taking an action or making a decision involving its elements

Rules Are Interesting When Unexpected

Unexpectedness requires a model of the user’s expectations and of the application domain.

Patterns can be classified by comparing:

  • Whether they were expected to be frequent or infrequent
  • Whether they actually turn out to be frequent or infrequent

The most interesting cases are mismatches:

  • Expected infrequent, but actually frequent
  • Expected frequent, but actually infrequent

Datasets With Inhomogeneous Support

Many datasets contain:

  • Itemsets with very high support
  • Itemsets with very low support

Example: a large commercial chain sells products priced from 1 euro to 10,000 euro.

  • Low-price products appear in many more transactions
  • High-price products appear in fewer transactions
  • Associations among high-price products may still be important

Datasets With Inhomogeneous Support: Threshold Choice

Setting minsup is difficult when item supports differ strongly.

If the threshold is too high:

  • Associations among low-support items are missed

If the threshold is too low:

  • Execution time increases
  • Too many rules are returned
  • Cross-support patterns become frequent

Cross-Support Pattern

A cross-support pattern is an itemset:

\[X=\{i_1,\ldots,i_k\}\]

where the support ratio \(r(X)\) is lower than a threshold thr.

\[r(X)=\frac{\min(s(i_1),\ldots,s(i_k))}{\max(s(i_1),\ldots,s(i_k))}\]

Example:

  • \(s(\text{milk})=70\%\)
  • \(s(\text{sugar})=10\%\)
  • \(s(\text{caviar})=0.04\%\)

If thr = 0.01, then \(\{\text{milk}, \text{sugar}, \text{caviar}\}\) is cross-support because:

\[0.0004/0.7 = 0.00058 < 0.01\]

Cross-Support Pattern: Why It Matters

With threshold thr = 0.3, itemsets such as \(\{p,q,r\}\), \(\{p,r\}\), and \(\{p,q\}\) can become cross-support patterns.

The related association rules may have high confidence but little practical interest.

  • \(c(\{q\} \to \{p\})=4/5=80\%\)
  • \(s(\{p,q\})=4/30=13.3\%\)
  • \(s(\{q,r\})=5/30=16.7\%\)
  • \(c(\{p\} \to \{q\})=4/25=16\%\)

Support and confidence alone may not capture correlation or affinity among itemsets.

Handling Categorical and Continuous Attributes

In the basic formulation, association rules work with binary asymmetric variables.

  • An item appears or does not appear in a transaction
  • The relevant information is presence, not absence

How can we express rules such as:

\[\{\text{Number of Pages} \in [5,10) \land \text{Browser}=\text{Firefox}\} \to \{\text{Buy}=\text{No}\}\]

Handling Categorical Attributes

Binarization transforms categorical attributes into asymmetric binary attributes by introducing a new item for every possible attribute value.

For example, Browser Type becomes:

  • Browser Type = Internet Explorer
  • Browser Type = Firefox
  • Browser Type = Chrome

When the number of attribute values is high, this has a heavy impact on performance.

Handling Continuous Attributes

Association rules that include continuous values are called quantitative association rules.

Example:

\[\text{Age} \in [21,35) \land \text{Salary} \in [70k,120k) \to \text{Buy}\]

Continuous attributes can be handled using several approaches:

  • Based on discretization
  • Based on statistics
  • Without discretization, such as minApriori

Handling Continuous Attributes: Discretization

Discretization raises the problem of how to choose the number and borders of intervals.

The number of intervals is usually supplied by the user and can be expressed in terms of:

  • Interval range: equi-width discretization
  • Average number of transactions per interval: equi-depth or equi-frequency discretization
  • Number of clusters

Interval width affects support and confidence:

  • Too large intervals reduce confidence
  • Too narrow intervals reduce support and tend to produce replicated rules

Handling Continuous Attributes: Interval Width

Consider a dataset with two patterns:

  • \(R_1: \text{Age} \in [16,24) \to \text{Chat online}=Y\) with \(s=8.8\%\) and \(c=81.5\%\)
  • \(R_2: \text{Age} \in [44,60) \to \text{Chat online}=N\) with \(s=16.8\%\) and \(c=81.5\%\)

Using minsup = 5% and minconf = 65%:

  • Large intervals reduce confidence because groups no longer capture homogeneous behavior
  • Narrow intervals reduce support because each group contains fewer transactions

Examples with large intervals:

  • \(R_1: \text{Age} \in [12,36) \to \text{Chat online}=Y\) with \(s=30\%\) and \(c=57.7\%\)
  • \(R_2: \text{Age} \in [36,60) \to \text{Chat online}=N\) with \(s=28\%\) and \(c=58.3\%\)

Handling Continuous Attributes: Brute Force

One possible solution is to try all possible intervals.

  • Start from an initial range size \(k\)
  • Gradually fuse intervals
  • Execution time can become prohibitive
  • The solution can generate many redundant rules

Examples of redundant rules:

  • \(\{\text{Refund}=\text{No}, \text{Income}=\$51{,}250\} \to \{\text{Cheat}=\text{No}\}\)
  • \(\{\text{Refund}=\text{No}, 51K \leq \text{Income} \leq 52K\} \to \{\text{Cheat}=\text{No}\}\)
  • \(\{\text{Refund}=\text{No}, 50K \leq \text{Income} \leq 60K\} \to \{\text{Cheat}=\text{No}\}\)

|-- Housewares
|-- TV
|-- DVD
|-- Printers
|-- Scanner
`-- Accessories

Multi-Level Association Rules: Why Hierarchies Help

Hierarchies can be incorporated because:

  • Rules at lower levels may not have enough support to appear in frequent itemsets
  • Rules at lower levels may be too specific

Examples:

  • Skim milk \(\to\) white bread
  • Partly skim milk \(\to\) white bread
  • Skim milk \(\to\) whole wheat bread

These can all indicate a more general association between milk and bread.

Multi-Level Association Rules: Extended Transactions

Multi-level association rules can be handled by extending each transaction with parent items.

Original transaction:

\[\{\text{skim milk}, \text{white bread}\}\]

Extended transaction:

\[\{\text{skim milk}, \text{white bread}, \text{milk}, \text{bread}, \text{food}\}\]

The concept of association must be redefined to avoid useless associations.

  • \(X \Rightarrow Y\)
  • \(X \cap Y = \emptyset\)
  • \(Y\) must not contain an ancestor of any element of \(X\)

Consequences:

  • High-level items have very high support
  • Cross-support patterns may appear
  • The number of redundant associations increases
